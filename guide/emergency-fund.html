Guide / Emergency fund

Resilience

Emergency savings buys time and better choices.

A reserve is not designed to maximize return. Its job is to keep a surprise from becoming high-interest debt or a forced investment sale.

Size the reserve around risk

There is no single correct number of months for every household. Start with essential monthly expenses, then adjust for job stability, number of earners, health needs, dependents, insurance deductibles, home ownership and access to other liquid resources.

Lower riskStable dual income

A smaller reserve may be workable when income is diversified and fixed obligations are modest.

Moderate riskOne primary income

A larger reserve can provide more recovery time after job loss or a major expense.

Higher riskVariable income or high obligations

Self-employment, commissions, health uncertainty or high fixed costs can justify a larger liquidity buffer.

ShareCapsule opinion

Use three months of essential expenses as a checkpoint, not a finish line. For many households, building toward roughly six months is a stronger long-term resilience target; some situations justify more.

Emergency fund vs. sinking fund

FundPurposeExamples
Emergency fundUnexpected, urgent, financially disruptive eventsJob loss, urgent travel, major uninsured expense, emergency repair.
Sinking fundPredictable but irregular future spendingCar tires, property tax, annual insurance, holiday travel, tuition payment.

If a cost is predictable, planning for it separately keeps the emergency reserve available for genuine uncertainty.

Where to keep it

Liquid enough to access without selling volatile assets.
Separate enough from daily spending that it is not casually consumed.
At an insured financial institution when applicable and within relevant coverage limits.
Reviewed after major changes in housing, employment, insurance or family size.
